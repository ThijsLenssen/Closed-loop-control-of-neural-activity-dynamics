{\Large\bfseries Cortical beta oscillations map to shared brain networks modulated by dopamine\par}

\textbf{Paper:} Chikermane et al. (2024).\quad Human recordings and network mapping.\par
\href{https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.97184}{DOI: \nolinkurl{10.7554/eLife.97184}}\par
{\footnotesize Page references below use PDF page numbers, including front matter.}

\question{To which general question does this paper relate?}
Measurement and the network basis of beta activity. Useful for deciding what a beta measurement represents before selecting a feedback objective.

\question{How does it answer the general questions?}
\textbf{Measured.} Recordings selected from clinically normal cortical regions in epilepsy patients are combined with normative connectivity and dopamine-related PET maps. Regions with prominent beta activity share connectivity with a distributed cortico-subcortical network (PDF pp.~1--9).

\textbf{Interpretation.} Beta is also a physiological rhythm associated with several functions. The spatial correspondence with dopamine markers supports a network hypothesis; it does not demonstrate that beta causes symptoms or that suppressing it improves behaviour. This is not a DBS treatment trial.

\textbf{Model relevance.} Supports considering cortical inputs and distributed coupling rather than assigning beta exclusively to one nucleus. The connectivity maps do not identify dynamical coupling gains, transmission delays, or a firing-rate-to-LFP measurement equation for the notebook model (PDF pp.~8--12).
