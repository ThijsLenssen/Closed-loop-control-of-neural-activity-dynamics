                                                    Electrical neurostimulation is effective in the treatment of neurological
                                                    disorders, but associated recording artefacts generally limit its applications
                                                    to open-loop stimuli. Real-time and continuous closed-loop control of
                                                    brain activity can, however, be achieved by pairing concurrent electrical
                                                    recordings and optogenetics. Here we show that closed-loop optogenetic
                                                    stimulation with excitatory opsins enables the precise manipulation of
                                                    neural dynamics in brain slices from transgenic mice and in anaesthetized
                                                    non-human primates. The approach generates oscillations in quiescent tissue,
                                                    enhances or suppresses endogenous patterns in active tissue and modulates
                                                    seizure-like bursts elicited by the convulsant 4-aminopyridine. A nonlinear
                                                    model of the phase-dependent effects of optical stimulation reproduced
                                                    the modulation of cycles of local-field potentials associated with seizure
                                                    oscillations, as evidenced by the systematic changes in the variability and
                                                    entropy of the phase-space trajectories of seizures, which correlated with
                                                    changes in their duration and intensity. We also show that closed-loop o­pt­
                                                    og­en­etic n­eu­rostimulation could be delivered using intracortical optrodes
                                                    incorporating light-emitting diodes. Closed-loop optogenetic approaches
                                                    may be translatable to therapeutic applications in humans.

Life sciences study design
All studies must disclose on these points even when the disclosure is negative.
  Sample size              Statistical tests were performed within subject (that is, a brain slice or a non-human primate), recording data for as long as the 4AP-elicited
                           seizure activity could be maintained (typically, > 2 hours). As reported in the text, our sample size was 21 (endogenous activity) and 10
                           (seizure activity) transgenic brain-slice experiments. For the seizure-suppression experiments, we obtained a significant effect in 8/10 within-
                           slice comparisons as well as a significant effect at the population level. Thus, we are confident that this is a sufficient dataset to support our
                           claims. We added a new dataset of 3 mice replicating these results using viral expression of opsins. Since these experiments required recovery
                           surgery and therefore incurred a higher welfare cost, and given the reproducibility of our transgenic experiments, we chose n = 3. In vivo
                           experiments with non-human primates used two animals, in order to demonstrate replication. It is standard practice to use n = 2 when using
                           non-human primates in neuroscience, owing to the ethical costs of larger sample sizes. Statistically significant effects were obtained within
                           each hemisphere in each animal. See https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.05.10.491373v1.full.pdf for a discussion of sample-size
                           considerations in NHPs.

  Data exclusions          Datasets from all in vivo experiments (Supplementary Table 1) were included in the analyses, and no data points were excluded. Datasets
                           were only excluded from the in vitro seizure-experiment analyses in cases where 4-AP did not elicit epileptiform activity.

  Replication              Seizure modulation was replicated in 10 brain-slice experiments with transgenic mice. We also performed an independent replication of these
                           results in a second series of slice experiments that used 3 animals with viral expression of virus. In addition, we performed a series of in vivo
                           experiments with 3 brain hemispheres from 2 non-human primates (the fourth hemisphere was not injected with virus and was not tested).
                           Statistically significant effects were obtained in 3/3 hemispheres in non-human primates. Thus, our results were replicated across different
                           opsin-expression techniques and two species.

  Randomization            We used a within-subject study design to test the effect of a closed-loop phase-shift. Within each experiment, the presentation of different
                           phase conditions was pseudorandomised. All statistical tests are based on comparisons between different phase conditions.

  Blinding                 No blinding was required for our within-subject study design.
